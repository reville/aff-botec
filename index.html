Addiction Frontier Fund BOTEC

A development-funding model for better addiction medicines

1

Novel drug classes can dramatically change the size of a behavioral health treatment market.

Before the modern GLP-1 expansion: about 1 in 100. In 2012–2016, about 1% of US adults with obesity used an obesity medicine in an average year: 660,000 people out of 71.6 million. GAO

In the GLP-1 era: nearly 1 in 4. In late 2025, 23% of US adults reporting a diagnosis of overweight or obesity within the previous five years said they were currently taking a GLP-1. KFF, November 2025

Obesity is the clearest recent analogue for the Addiction Frontier Fund: a condition with high unmet need closely linked to behavior, where a more effective therapeutic class expanded a small medication market.

2

The model

QALYs = portfolio programs × P(approval) × QALYs/yr per medicine × years of benefit × credit share × capital recycled

Then: philanthropic $/QALY = total fund cost ÷ QALYs, and Coefficient-style multiple = $100,000 ÷ philanthropic $/QALY.

Every cost-per-QALY figure the chain produces is a funder-perspective number: AFF's own dollars divided by the share of health gain credited to them. It is the leverage metric Open Philanthropy uses for its science grants, where the funder pays a slice of development and payers later buy the medicine. It is not the payer-perspective cost-effectiveness of the medicine itself, which divides the drug's price and delivery cost by the health it buys and is judged against thresholds such as the $104k US figure. The GiveWell comparison is fair because a GiveWell charity pays for the whole intervention, so its two perspectives coincide. Comparing $160 with the price-based cost-effectiveness of an existing medicine is not, and the Benchmarking section keeps the two in separate tables.

Assumptions behind the investment case

$250MFull cost of the fund

The model charges all $250M, including the $70M outside direct portfolio development.

What determines the result. These results are development-funding cost per health gain. They do not include every payer’s future medicine, delivery or implementation costs. A full intervention cost-effectiveness analysis would add those costs.

~$20MAFF capital per program

About $180M in development capital divided by $20M per program funds nine first-round programs. The remaining development capital must come from other investors or partners. [9][10]

What determines the result. As the obesity case illustrates, commercial financing can expand with demonstrated value. The timing is uncertain. If AFF must carry programs further before partners enter, fewer programs fit within the fund.

20%Probability of approval

The base case uses 20% per program and ~13% probability that nine independent programs yield no approval. Psychiatry and neurology development data inform this judgment. [12]

What determines the result. A large latent market is not evidence that a molecule will work. Asset stage, human evidence, safety and clinical endpoints must determine approval probability. Shared mechanisms can also make failures correlated.

75kAnnual health benefit per approved medicine

The base combines additional treatment coverage with the benefit delivered per person. The illustrative coverage increases are +5 points in alcohol, +5 in opioids, +4 in tobacco and +3 for repurposed products.

What determines the result. Measure incremental users, time effectively treated and benefit relative to the alternative. The calculator combines these into annual QALYs, rather than offering independent uptake and persistence controls.

15 yearsDiscounted-equivalent benefit duration

The 15-year parameter approximates the discounted stream of benefit attributable to the fund, including ramp-up and acceleration. [8]

What determines the result. A medicine’s adoption curve develops over time, as the obesity precedent shows. Development lag, the pace of uptake, duration of clinical benefit and the arrival of competing treatments determine the benefit over time. The 15-year parameter combines these effects.

50%Counterfactual credit

Half the modeled impact is attributed to AFF. Moving this assumption from 50% to 40% worsens cost per QALY by 1.25×, holding the rest fixed.

What determines the result. As a market becomes more attractive, private investment may follow and AFF’s additionality may fall. Assess the candidate and indication with and without AFF. Approval probability and additionality may be correlated; multiplying portfolio-wide averages can misstate the result.

1.5×Capital recycling

The model assumes returns fund additional programs, increasing total impact by 1.5×. Venture-philanthropy precedents motivate including this channel. [10][20]

What determines the result. Recycling depends on exits, contractual rights and timing. It should eventually be modeled conditionally on success and delayed reinvestment.

1×–15×Geographic reach

The four geographic views count 1×, 2×, 5× and 15× US health impact. The default 5× case is conditional on substantial non-US benefit.

What determines the result. Plan for price, licensing, manufacturing, registration and delivery by product and country. Disease burden sets the opportunity; the access plan and observed uptake determine how much of it becomes benefit.

3%Discounting and comparison

The model uses 3% discounting within its effective duration convention. Its QALYs and global-health DALY benchmarks are different health measures.

What determines the result. Comparisons between funding opportunities depend on consistent health units, discount rates, timing and cost boundaries. This BOTEC measures health gains in QALYs and counts development funding as the cost; a DALY benchmark or a full treatment-cost analysis uses different conventions.

QALYs/year calculation

Several precedents show that adoption in behavioral conditions can rise dramatically when superior drug classes become available. For addiction, the resulting health benefit depends on how many additional people receive effective treatment, how long they benefit, and how much their health improves compared with the care they would otherwise receive.

The base case assumes 75,000 QALYs per year per approved medicine. This is the most influential and uncertain input; the calculation combines additional effective treatment with the health benefit per patient:

QALYs/yr = incremental patients on effective treatment × (baseline mortality × risk reduction × QALYs per death averted + morbidity QALYs per patient-year)

For example, an OUD adherence medicine: ~5M with opioid use disorder × ~5 points of incremental coverage ≈ 250k patients; × (3.6%/yr untreated mortality × 55% reduction [14] × ~9 QALYs per death averted + ~0.08 morbidity QALY/yr) ≈ ~65,000 QALYs/yr. The 75k base sits just above this; the 450k High is the polysubstance GLP-1 in the last row.

8

What the obesity precedent implies for addiction

A better therapeutic class can change the decision to seek and continue treatment. The uptake of today’s medicines is evidence about those products in their current setting. It is not a fixed measure of willingness to reduce drinking, quit smoking or avoid drug-related harm.

Build the forecast around a specified product

A medicine that delivers a noticeable reduction in craving, an acceptable burden of side effects and convenient treatment may reach people who would decline existing options. Some people may seek reduced consumption or harm before they seek abstinence. Those are possibilities to test through patient research and trials, rather than reasons to exclude those people from the potential market.

Define the intended benefit, dosing, delivery route, safety monitoring and price before projecting adoption. The successful obesity medicines show that patients can accept treatment burdens when the benefit matters enough; the target is a worthwhile overall experience, not an arbitrary requirement such as weekly dosing for every product.

Separate indications and treatment settings

Opioid-treatment experience informs opioid forecasts. It cannot determine demand for every other addictive indication.

Follow demand through the treatment pathway

Measure willingness to try the specified medicine, access to prescribing and supply, initiation, effective time on treatment, restarting, and health outcomes. Removing one administrative barrier tests that barrier in the existing treatment system. A limited change in prescribing after its removal does not establish that all access barriers are irrelevant or that a better medicine would have no demand.

For each program, set uptake and persistence milestones against a defined price and delivery setting. Revise the forecast if willingness, initiation or benefit falls short. Limited prescribing alone cannot distinguish weak demand from blocked access; the pathway data should show where the shortfall occurs.

9

Tier 2: the downstream social costs a medicine also averts

For addiction, health gains can also reduce burdens on families, work and public systems. Tier 1 counts health; Tier 2 separately estimates selected downstream dollar benefits. Those gains depend on the same realized treatment and clinical effects, so market growth alone is not enough.

This section adds a separate dollar ledger. The rule is simple: the two tiers never blend. Tier 1 stays in health units. Tier 2 stays in dollars. No Tier 2 dollar is converted into a QALY, divided by a cost-effectiveness threshold, or added to the health benefit before a benefit-cost ratio is struck. That is the two-reference-case structure the Second Panel on Cost-Effectiveness in Health and Medicine recommends [66].

Why the separation mattersThe most quoted cost figure in this literature is Florence's $221,219 per person with opioid use disorder per year [67]. Eighty-three percent of that number is monetized quality of life, valued at $517,324 per QALY. Quoting it beside a QALY count values the same suffering twice. Many advocacy documents do not separate the two.

From one case to the portfolio

Two scaling routes would overstate Tier 2. The first is patients reached multiplied by the full per-case cost, which assumes a medicine resolves the entire disorder while Tier 1 credits it with only a thin fraction of a QALY per patient-year. The two tiers would then describe different medicines, and Tier 2 would come out roughly four times too large. The second is deriving Tier 2 straight from health units. Tier 2 is denominated in real dollars, so the same fund must avert the same dollars whether this page reports QALYs or the other edition's units.

The model instead uses disorder case-years averted, which is unit-independent. Morbidity health units divided by the full per-case-year decrement in that unit system returns case-years; case-years multiplied by the Tier 2 cost per case-year returns dollars. The fraction of the disorder a medicine resolves cancels out of that ratio.

The bridge is computed in quality-adjusted life-years no matter which unit this edition reports, because the cost study supplying the decrement is itself QALY-denominated. Mixing a GBD disability weight into a Florence-derived cost would be a unit error of exactly the kind this section exists to avoid.

Only morbidity units convert. Averted deaths carry no Tier 2 dollars at all, deliberately: a person whose death is averted goes on living, and if still in disorder goes on generating criminal-justice and productivity costs rather than avoiding them. Crediting Tier 2 savings to averted deaths would have the wrong sign. This is a real conservatism and it removes roughly two thirds of the health tier from the Tier 2 calculation.

Who gets the money back

The total accrues to different payers, so a payer split is more useful. Per case-year of opioid use disorder, of $21,668 in Tier 2 costs:

  • Taxpayers, about $12,000 (56%). Criminal justice is 98.9% publicly borne, worth $6,884; forgone tax revenue on lost earnings adds about $5,162.
  • Private parties, about $9,600 (44%). Individuals, families and employers.

Sensitivity on the taxpayer line runs from $6,900 (32%, counting avoided government outlays only) through $9,800 (45%) to $12,000 (56%). We say taxpayer share rather than government savings throughout, because roughly $5,200 of the central figure is revenue that was never collected rather than money that was spent. Corroborating top-down anchors: government pays 40.4% of the $249B annual cost of excessive drinking [71], and bore 29% of the $631B four-year opioid total measured by the Society of Actuaries, or 49% once mortality costs are excluded, which they must be here because this model already counts deaths as health units [72].

The honest counterThis split is an analyst construct, not a published figure. Nobody has published a payer decomposition of Florence's per-case components; these shares come from the alcohol payer table that underlies the CDC cost series [71], applied to opioid components. The 32% to 56% spread is driven by one methodological choice, whether the tax wedge on lost earnings counts as a taxpayer benefit, and the reader should assume the true figure sits inside that range rather than at its top.

The precedent

Prior studies have found this pattern. Fairley et al. modeled US opioid use disorder treatment under two perspectives [75]. Counting health-sector costs only, medication treatment was cost-effective but not cost-saving, at about $16,000 per QALY gained for methadone against no treatment. Adding one downstream category, criminal justice, made every form of medication treatment cost-saving, with discounted lifetime savings of $25,000 to $105,000 per person treated. Health-care spending rose from about $256,000 to between $272,000 and $314,000 per person over a lifetime; criminal-justice spending fell by more, from about $357,000 to between $212,000 and $279,000. The authors excluded lost productivity, child welfare and housing, and noted that including them would make treatment look better still. Krebs et al. found the same pattern in California, where immediate access to opioid agonist treatment cost $78,257 less per person and produced 0.42 more QALYs, with 99.6% of two thousand simulations giving both lower costs and better health [76].

A note on that rangeFairley's abstract and Table 2 give $25,000 to $105,000; the discussion section of the same paper says approximately $15,000 to $90,000, and no correction has been published. We cite the table.

Europe and the world

Tier 2 dollars above are United States figures and are not multiplied by this page's deployment-scope scenarios. Transplanting US per-case costs and US price levels onto other health systems would be indefensible, and the burden ratios differ by substance in any case: global-to-US addiction mortality runs about 16.7× for tobacco and 14.6× for alcohol but only about 7.5× for drugs, and Tier 2 is drug and alcohol weighted rather than tobacco weighted. Three separate ledgers, never one world total:

  • United States. Dollars and health units, as above.
  • European Union. Alcohol-led and method-based rather than borrowed: at 1.5% to 2.6% of GDP against EU-27 output of €16.9 trillion, alcohol costs the EU roughly €254B to €439B a year [77]. Illicit drugs are reported in deaths and health units only, because no credible EU-wide cost total exists; the EU records about 7,600 drug-induced deaths a year on a narrow registry definition that is not comparable to the US count. Two differences matter in Europe: opioid agonist treatment reaches about half of high-risk opioid users in the EU against roughly a quarter in the US, so the treatment-gap argument is weaker there, while alcohol's burden is proportionally larger. EU productivity costing also uses the friction-cost method, which produces materially lower totals than the human-capital method behind every US figure on this page.
  • Global. Health units only. Alcohol is associated with 2.6 million deaths and 116 million DALYs a year, drug use with about 600,000 deaths of which roughly 450,000 are opioid; 63 million people have a drug use disorder and about one in twelve receives treatment [78]. No global economic-cost estimate exists for illicit drugs, and this page does not construct one. Where global figures are used, the US share is netted out, because high-income North America is roughly half the world's opioid-use-disorder burden and would otherwise be counted twice.

The full distribution

The Low and High columns above are scenario corners. The Monte Carlo instead samples each input as a lognormal centered on its Base value with the tabled spread, and samples the number of approvals as a discrete binomial draw (9 programs × 20%) rather than a smooth 1.8. The chain's ~$160 is a central estimate, every input at its base value; it is not the mean of this distribution, and the two differ for a reason set out below.

About … of sampled portfolios produce no approval at all. Among portfolios with at least one approval, the median is … and the 90% interval is …. The zero-approval share runs above the ~13% in the binomial table because the simulation also samples the approval probability and the program count; averaging over that spread raises the chance of an empty portfolio. Across all draws, zero-approval portfolios included, the mean of the simulated QALYs implies … per QALY, better than the ~$160 central estimate because every input is right-skewed and a product of skewed inputs has a mean well above its median. … of those expected QALYs come from the top 10% of draws. What could prevent the opportunity becoming impact

  • The product does not offer enough improvement. Safety, efficacy and patient experience must be established in the intended population.
  • Demand does not become effective use. Cost, supply, prescribing, side effects or limited perceived benefit can interrupt treatment. Measure each stage rather than treating all non-use as lack of demand.
  • Use shifts without much extra benefit. Switching from an existing treatment counts only to the extent that outcomes improve. Increased prescriptions are not automatically additional health gains.
  • Commercial investment would deliver the same result. As a market becomes more attractive, AFF may become less additional. Update the counterfactual for each program and its access strategy.
  • The global route does not materialize. The 5× impact case needs more than approval in the US. Products with limited affordability or delivery capacity belong in lower-reach scenarios.
  • The portfolio fails or produces net harm. The independent-program base includes ~13% probability of no approval. Correlated failures and adverse clinical outcomes remain risks.

Questions that determine the investment case

Why might this be an undercount?
  • The model assumes much less market expansion than the obesity and antidepressant precedents. The base case adds 3 to 5 percentage points of treatment coverage per indication. The obesity comparison spans about 0.9% annual medication use among adults with obesity in 2012–2016 and 23% current GLP-1 use among adults reporting overweight or obesity in late 2025. Antidepressant use likewise expanded in the SSRI era: 2.5% in 1988–1994 in an age-adjusted estimate for people aged 17 and over, compared with 13.2% of adults in 2015–2018. A similarly successful addiction medicine could reach many more people than this base case assumes.
  • Tier 1 counts health gains in the people treated. Substance use is social behavior: when one person cuts back or quits, friends, partners and peers may follow as shared routines and expectations change. This can create a viral effect, with reductions spreading beyond the people receiving medicine. Studies of smoking cessation and alcohol consumption support investigating these spillovers. The model adds no health benefit for this additional reduction in use. Tier 1 also does not separately estimate health harms averted in other people, including secondhand smoke, alcohol-related traffic deaths and violence, and exposure to alcohol or drugs during pregnancy. Those health gains are distinct from Tier 2’s selected dollar benefits.
  • One 20% approval rate can understate the strongest opportunities. The portfolio model applies the same 20% probability to all nine programs. Funding an AUD trial of a psychedelic already in phase 3 for depression, backed by positive human efficacy results, would build on an existing safety database, dosing work and manufacturing. Much of the early development has already been done in a related indication. For a well-selected asset with encouraging AUD evidence and a clear regulatory pathway, we would expect approval odds substantially above 20% and a route to market potentially years shorter than an early-stage program. Randomized psilocybin results in AUD support this strategy; approval for AUD still requires its own convincing trials. Earlier launch increases discounted health benefit and can return capital for reinvestment sooner.
Why have existing addiction drugs had limited uptake and impact?

Many existing medicines offer benefits that patients weigh against side effects and the burden of treatment. Limited prescribing, coverage and access further reduce their reach. The main approved drugs illustrate several different constraints:

  • Alcohol: modest average benefits and tolerability problems. Oral naltrexone helps reduce heavy drinking, but a 2023 meta-analysis found that about 11 people needed treatment to prevent one additional person from returning to heavy drinking compared with placebo. Acamprosate also had a number needed to treat of about 11 for preventing a return to any drinking, without a demonstrated reduction in return to heavy drinking. Naltrexone increases nausea and vomiting; acamprosate increases diarrhea and usually requires six tablets across three daily doses. JAMA review; acamprosate label. Disulfiram deters drinking by causing an unpleasant, sometimes dangerous reaction to alcohol, making adherence and willingness to take it central limitations. Disulfiram label.
  • Smoking: even the strongest approved medicines leave most people smoking. Cochrane estimates that about 21–25 in 100 people using varenicline quit for at least six months, versus about 10 in 100 with placebo. That is a meaningful improvement, but most do not achieve sustained abstinence in a given attempt. At the standard full dose, varenicline’s label reports nausea in 30% versus 10% with placebo; insomnia and abnormal dreams are also more common. Bupropion and single-form nicotine replacement are less effective than varenicline; bupropion commonly causes insomnia and dry mouth. Cochrane; varenicline label; FDA.
  • Opioids: effective medicines can still be difficult to start and stay on. Methadone and buprenorphine suppress illicit opioid use and substantially reduce mortality during treatment. Constipation, sweating and sleepiness can make treatment burdensome. Methadone’s US outpatient dispensing requirements constrain access, while starting buprenorphine too soon after other opioids can precipitate withdrawal. For these medicines, initiation and retention are major opportunities to improve population impact. Mortality evidence; methadone; buprenorphine.
  • Injectable naltrexone: the treatment can work once started, but getting started is a hurdle. For opioid use disorder, patients must first complete withdrawal before initiating this opioid blocker. In the X:BOT trial, 72% successfully started injectable naltrexone versus 94% starting buprenorphine–naloxone. Difficulty initiating naltrexone led to more relapse overall; among those who started treatment, relapse outcomes were similar. Injection-site reactions add another burden. X:BOT randomized trial.

Better medicines could expand the market by delivering larger benefits, fewer side effects or an easier treatment experience. Today’s uptake reflects the products and treatment systems available today.

Does low use of existing medicines show that a new medicine will go unused?

It establishes the current treatment level, not the ceiling for a different product. The obesity comparison above shows a small market expanding with a more effective and appealing therapeutic class. Addiction forecasts should therefore be built around the specified product and its delivery conditions. The base case requires only single-digit percentage-point additions to coverage.

Does this assume stigma and access barriers disappear?

A product’s benefits and treatment experience can change willingness to seek care, while access still determines whether that willingness becomes use. A person declining current care does not reveal how they would respond to a substantially different treatment. In obesity, improved treatments have dramatically reduced stigma.

Does a one-year discontinuation rate erase the benefit?

Benefit depends on time effectively treated, response, restarting and any effects that persist after stopping. One-year status alone cannot recover that full trajectory. Use treatment and outcome data together, and avoid applying a second discontinuation adjustment to effects that already include it.

Why does the default include five times US impact?

It is a global diffusion scenario. With roughly 14 times the US burden outside the US, 5× total impact requires about 28.6% of US-equivalent benefit per unit of non-US burden.
